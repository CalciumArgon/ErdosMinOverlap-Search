Minimize  C_5 = max_k ∫ h(x)(1 − h(x+k)) dx  over h: [0, 2] → [0, 1]
with ∫_0^2 h(x) dx = 1, where h is represented as n_points samples on a uniform
grid of [0, 2] (cell width dx = 2 / n_points).
Do this by evolving the code between # EVOLVE-BLOCK-START and # EVOLVE-BLOCK-END.

## Output protocol (dense grid)

run_code() must return the 3-tuple (h_values, c5_bound, n_points):

- h_values: 1-D float array of length n_points, every entry in [0, 1].
- n_points: int, the number of grid cells (your choice; see the cost note below).
- c5_bound: float, YOUR OWN value of
      max( np.correlate(h_values, 1 - h_values, mode="full") * (2.0 / n_points) )
  computed on the final h_values you return — not a target you were aiming for.

Feasibility is checked exactly, do not rely on tolerance:
- sum(h_values) must equal n_points / 2 EXACTLY (this is ∫h = 1 on the grid).
  If it does not, the evaluator rescales h_values proportionally and then compares
  its recomputed C5 against your c5_bound with atol=1e-8; a rescale makes that
  comparison fail and the candidate is discarded as invalid.
- Every entry must stay in [0, 1] both before and after any rescale.
- n_points must be a positive int and equal len(h_values).

Cost note: the correlation is O(n_points^2) work in float64 and your program runs
under a 1100-second evaluation budget that also has to cover everything your
run_code() does (construction, local search, any self-checks you add). Larger
n_points buys resolution; it does not by itself buy a better or more trustworthy
score.

## What the score means — read this before optimizing

The evaluator recomputes C5 independently from h_values and ignores any score you
report except as a cross-check. The number it computes is exact (up to float
rounding) for the grid function you returned: on a uniform grid the sliding-window
objective M(s) = ∫ h(x)(1 − h(x+s)) dx is piecewise linear in the continuous shift
s, with slope changes only at integer multiples of dx, so its maximum over all
real shifts is attained at one of the integer shifts that mode="full" enumerates.
Consequence: score differences between candidates are real signal about the
candidates you submitted, not sampling noise, and the search is not being fooled
by a lucky shift grid.

But be clear about WHICH quantity that score bounds. The grid function you return
is a two-stage relaxation of the original problem:

1. Value relaxation: h takes values in [0, 1], not just {0, 1}. The original
   combinatorial object is a subset A of [0, 2] with |A| = 1 and h = 1_A.
2. Discretization: h is constant on n_points fixed cells instead of being an
   arbitrary function of a continuum of points.

Let C*_relax be the infimum over the relaxed class and C*_bin over indicator
functions. Every indicator function is also a [0,1]-valued function, so
C*_relax <= C*_bin, and the reverse inequality is not established here. A lower
score therefore lowers a certified upper bound on C*_relax. It does NOT by itself
certify closeness to the binary / continuum ground truth, and no value of
n_points alone supplies such a bound.

Two specific ways a low score can be misleading:

- Grid-scale structure. A construction whose benefit comes from features at the
  scale of dx can change materially when the grid is refined, because the
  underlying function is not stable under refinement.
- Fractional smoothing. Intermediate values can reduce the max-overlap in a way
  that no equal-mass {0,1}-valued function reproduces; that gain belongs to the
  relaxation, not to the original problem.

You are free to decide how much weight to give these considerations. What is
asked is only that you not treat the score as if it were a bound on the original
problem when it is not.

## Judging your own candidates (optional, your call)

Nothing here is required, and none of it is a target to optimize toward — these
are ways to form your own judgment about whether a score you produced is
trustworthy:

- Refinement probe: re-score np.repeat(h_values, 2) at 2*n_points (mass is
  preserved automatically: sum doubles and so does n_points). If C5 moves a lot,
  the candidate's advantage was tied to the grid scale.
- Binarization probe: compare C5 of h_values against C5 of a {0,1}-valued array
  of the same length with the same total mass (n_points/2 ones), for instance one
  built from the largest entries of h_values. The gap says how much of the
  candidate's score depends on intermediate values.
- Self-consistency: recompute c5_bound with the exact formula above on the final
  array you return, after any projection or rounding step, so the cross-check
  passes.

A candidate whose score survives these probes is evidence about the problem; a
candidate whose score does not is evidence about the grid. Which kind you want to
submit, and how you trade that against the raw score, is your decision.

Fixed by the harness (do not try to change it): the objective, the feasibility

extraction.

Open to you: n_points, how h_values is constructed, what search or construction
